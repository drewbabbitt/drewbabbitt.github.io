What to expect

A private process, start to finish

Most people arrive knowing that something is happening and very little else, and they would rather not have to ask around to find out. So here is all of it written down: the common types of hair loss and which of them a system actually suits, then what happens at each appointment, how a system is built and matched to you, what it is made of, and what keeping it looks like month to month.

Nothing here is a diagnosis. It is the vocabulary, so that when you sit down with us, or with your doctor, you already know what is being talked about and what to ask.

Discretion

Why there are no client photographs on this website

Our clients are not on this site, and they are not going to be. Hair loss is private. A great many of the people we look after have not told the people closest to them, and a photograph is the one thing that cannot be taken back once it is online. Asking someone to trade that for our advertising is not something we are willing to do.

So this page does the job a gallery normally does. Instead of showing you other people's heads, we will show you the process, the materials, the construction and the upkeep, in as much detail as we can put in writing.

How a hairline sits, how the hair moves, how the density reads in daylight, all of that is easier to see on your own head, in the mirror, in the studio. Come in and we will put the actual materials in your hands. If you come to us, the same protection is yours.

The basics

How hair grows, and what “losing it” actually means

Every follicle on your head runs its own cycle, independently of its neighbours. It grows a hair for a long stretch, briefly winds that production down, rests, sheds, and starts again. At any given moment the large majority of your follicles are in the growing phase, which is why healthy scalps shed hair every day without ever looking thinner.

The three phases of the hair growth cycle, drawn in cross-section through the scalp. Original schematics, not photographs of any person.

Almost everything that gets called “hair loss” is one of three things happening to that cycle, and knowing which one you are dealing with is the single most useful thing you can learn.

Shedding, miniaturization and scarring, in one line each

Shedding

Too many follicles are pushed out of the growing phase at once, so far more hair than usual is shed over a few months. The follicles themselves are intact.

Miniaturization

Follicles keep cycling but produce progressively finer, shorter, lighter hair each time, until what grows is barely visible. This is the mechanism behind pattern hair loss.

Scarring

Inflammation destroys the follicle and scar tissue replaces it. Once a follicle is gone it does not come back, which is why this category is the one to get in front of quickly.

Types of hair loss

The presentations we are asked about most

For each one, the honest answer about whether a non-surgical solution is the right response, including the cases where it is a temporary bridge and the cases where we should tell you to wait.

01

Androgenetic alopecia

Male pattern hair loss. Genetic, gradual, and it moves in a predictable order.

Male pattern hair loss, and the most common cause of hair loss in men. A genetic predisposition combines with the follicle's sensitivity to androgens, and the result is progressive miniaturization: normal hairs are replaced, cycle after cycle, by finer and shorter ones until the scalp shows through.

It becomes more common with age and it moves in a predictable order. Recession at the temples first, then thinning at the crown, and eventually the two areas meet. The rim above the ears and around the back of the head is typically spared. It is non-scarring, it is gradual, and it does not reverse on its own.

Where a system fits: this is the strongest case for one. The pattern is stable and predictable, and the perimeter rim gives a fitted system real hair to blend into.

02

Female pattern hair loss

Diffuse thinning across the top and crown, with the part quietly widening.

The same underlying mechanism, presenting differently. Rather than a receding hairline, the loss is diffuse across the top and crown, and the frontal hairline is usually preserved. For most women the first sign is the part widening, or a ponytail that has quietly got thinner over a few years. Non-scarring, and gradual enough that it is often noticed late.

Where a system fits: a topper is the usual answer. Density is added exactly where it has gone, at the crown and along the part, while your own hair is kept at the sides and blended through.

03

Alopecia areata

Autoimmune. Well defined round patches that can appear within weeks.

An autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the follicle. It produces well-defined round patches of loss that can appear over a matter of weeks, and it can extend to the whole scalp or the whole body. The follicle is not destroyed, and the course is genuinely unpredictable: it can regrow, recur, or persist.

Where a system fits: full coverage is the common answer while it is active, and toppers or partial pieces work for limited patches. Because the picture can change, we fit for how things are now and plan on adjusting.

04

Telogen effluvium

A heavy shed months after illness, surgery, childbirth or severe stress.

A shock to the system pushes an unusually large number of follicles out of the growing phase and into resting all at once. Illness, surgery, childbirth, severe stress and some medications are the usual triggers. The shed shows up months after the event that caused it, which is why the cause is so often missed. It is diffuse over the whole scalp rather than patterned, and in most cases the follicles are intact and the hair returns.

Where a system fits: usually as a bridge rather than a permanent answer. Coverage while it recovers, with no expectation that you keep wearing one afterwards. We will tell you if that is what we think you are looking at.

05

Treatment-related loss

Chemotherapy and other medical treatment. Rapid, and often close to total.

Chemotherapy and some other treatments interrupt the growing phase directly, so the loss is rapid and can be close to total. The follicles are generally not destroyed and hair typically returns once treatment ends, though it can come back with a different texture or color for a while.

Where a system fits: full coverage, and where there is time, fitted before treatment starts so we can match your own color, density and hairline while they are still in front of us. See the section on cranial prostheses below.

06

Traction alopecia

From tight styles, extensions, or years of pull on the hairline.

Loss caused by sustained pull on the hair, from tight styles, extensions, or an attachment method worn hard for years. It shows up where the tension is highest, usually along the hairline and at the temples. Caught early it recovers once the pull stops. Left long enough, the follicle scars and the loss becomes permanent.

Where a system fits: only once the tension is off. This is the one presentation where the wrong attachment method makes things worse, so we choose it around your hairline, and we will say so plainly if what you are asking for is not right for your scalp.

07

Scarring alopecia

Cicatricial alopecia. Inflammation and scar tissue, and it is permanent.

A group of conditions, also called cicatricial alopecia, in which inflammation destroys the follicle and replaces it with scar tissue. Affected scalp often looks smooth or shiny with no visible follicle openings. What has been lost does not come back, so the priority is a dermatologist and getting the underlying process stopped before it takes more ground.

Where a system fits: coverage for what has already gone, with attachment chosen carefully around affected skin. Bring your dermatologist's guidance to the consultation and we will work within it.

This is not a complete list and it is not a diagnostic tool. More than one of these can be happening at the same time, and several of them look alike in the early stages. If your doctor has described your hair loss using a staging scale, you may have heard the Norwood or Ludwig scales named; those are shorthand clinicians use to record what they are looking at, and they are the physician's instrument, not ours. We do not stage and we do not diagnose. If you do not yet know what is going on, a dermatologist or your primary care provider is the right first stop, and it is worth going before you spend money on anything.

Construction

How a system actually sits on your head

Almost nobody has seen this in cross-section, and it explains most of the questions people arrive with. Nothing here goes under the skin, and nothing here is surgical.

1 · The hair

Human hair or synthetic fiber, chosen for color, wave and how much heat styling you want to be able to do. Human hair behaves like your own and can be colored; synthetic holds a set style through weather and costs less.

2 · The knots

Each strand is tied into the base by hand, one at a time. Knot size and the angle the hair leaves the base at are what make a hairline look grown rather than printed.

3 · The base

The sheet the hair is knotted into, cut to a template of your head. Lace, monofilament and thin polyurethane all behave differently, which is the next section.

4 · The attachment

Tape, liquid adhesive, clips or an integration method holds the base to your skin or to your own hair, usually around the perimeter. Nothing is injected, implanted or sutured.

5 · Your own scalp

Follicles that have miniaturized sit under the system; the ones still producing hair around the sides and back keep growing, and are what the system is blended into.

Materials

Choosing a base, and how it attaches

Every base is a trade between how invisible it looks, how long it lasts and how much upkeep it asks of you. There is no best one, only the one that fits how you live. The lifespans below are the usual working ranges and they move a lot with wear, climate and how the system is cleaned.

Fine lace

Lace is a mesh, so scalp shows through it and knots at the hairline effectively disappear. It is also the most fragile of the three and the one that needs the gentlest handling. Swiss lace is finer and shorter lived than French lace.

Usually one to three months. Best for undetectability at the front.

Monofilament

A stronger mesh that still lets hair move naturally in any direction, so a part can be worn anywhere on it. The middle option in almost every respect, and the most common choice for people who want one system they do not have to baby.

Usually six months to a year. Best all-rounder.

Thin skin, or polyurethane

A very thin flexible membrane that reads as scalp and gives the smoothest tape bond, which makes it easy to apply and remove yourself. It breathes less than a mesh, so it can feel warmer in a Central Valley summer.

Varies widely with thickness. Best for a simple attachment routine.

How the base is attached to you

Tape, liquid adhesive, clips or integration

Tape

Straightforward and gentle to remove. Suits a maintenance rhythm where the system comes off regularly.

Liquid adhesive

The most secure hold and the longest wear between visits. Requires more care around the skin.

Clips

Attached to your existing hair, on and off at will. Not suitable where the hairline is fragile, because of the pull.

Integration

Your own hair is pulled through an open base and blended with the added hair. Only an option where there is enough of your own hair to work with.

Human hair and synthetic fiber can be built onto any of these, and many systems combine materials, for instance a lace front with a stronger base through the middle where the wear happens. We will put samples of each in your hands at the consultation, so you can feel the difference before you commit to it.

The journey

Six stages, and what happens in each

Timelines differ by person and by the kind of system you choose. This is the shape of it.

01

The consultation

About an hour, behind a closed door, and free. We look at your scalp and whatever hair you still have, talk about how the loss has progressed and how quickly, and go through what is realistic. You will be asked what you want back, which is a harder question than it sounds, and what your mornings need to look like.

Nothing is decided and nothing is sold in this appointment. Plenty of people come once, take the information home, and return months later.

Free. No obligation. Bring your questions.

02

Color and density matching

This is the step that decides whether a system reads as your hair or as a piece. We take a sample from the hair you have, and match four things against it: base color, the gray percentage, the highlight or lowlight pattern, and the wave. Then density, which is the count of hairs per square inch, and which is almost always set lower than people first ask for. Real heads are not uniformly thick, and a system built too dense is the most recognizable mistake in this industry.

Hairline design happens here too. Where your hairline sat at thirty is rarely where it should sit now, and a slightly irregular, slightly receded front looks far more like you than a straight one.

Matched under studio light and checked again at the fitting.

03

The template and the build

A template is taken of your head so the base is cut to your shape rather than to an average. That template, along with your color sample, density, wave and the base material you chose, is what the system is made to. Stock pieces can be fitted much faster and cost less; a custom build takes longer and comes back fitted to you.

Turnaround for a custom order is usually several weeks. If you need something sooner, say so at the consultation, because that changes what we recommend.

Custom order or stock, depending on budget and timing.

04

The fitting

The perimeter is trimmed to your template, the attachment method is set up, and the system goes on for the first time. If you are using tape or adhesive we walk you through the exact products, where they go, and how much is too much. If you are using clips or an integration method, we show you the seat and the tension.

You will do it yourself before you leave. Nobody should walk out of a first fitting without having put their own system on and taken it off at least once.

Attachment, application and removal, taught in the chair.

05

The cut-in

A system out of the box is not finished. It is cut into your face and blended into the hair you still have while it is on your head, which is why the cut-in cannot be done in advance and cannot be done by someone who has not seen you wear it. Perimeter, sides, the transition at the temples, and the way the front falls all get set here.

Color is corrected at this stage too if the match needs a final adjustment against your own regrowth.

Included with a new fitting.

06

Living with it

You leave able to wash it, style it, sleep in it or take it off at night, depending on the attachment you chose. We send you with the products you need and written instructions, not a verbal list you have to remember in the car.

Call if something is not sitting right. Small problems in the first couple of weeks are normal and almost all of them are a five-minute fix.

Then maintenance every four to six weeks.

Maintenance

The rhythm after the first fitting

A system that is serviced on schedule keeps looking the way it did the day it went on. One that is not, does not, and that is the whole difference.

1

Every four to six weeks

A service visit. The system comes off, the base and your scalp are cleaned properly, the attachment is renewed, and the hair is reconditioned and re-cut where it needs it.

2

Between visits

You wash and style it yourself with the products we send you home with. Most people settle into a routine within a couple of weeks and stop thinking about it.

3

As your own hair changes

Loss continues underneath. Color is adjusted for regrowth and graying, and the blend is re-cut so the transition into your own hair stays honest.

4

Replacement

Every system eventually wears out, on a schedule set by its base and by you. Some clients keep a second one in rotation so there is never a day without. We plan the next one before the current one is finished.

Medical hair loss

Cranial prostheses and insurance

This section applies only to hair loss caused by a medical condition or its treatment, such as alopecia areata, chemotherapy and other treatment-related loss, and scarring alopecia. Pattern hair loss is treated by insurers as cosmetic and is generally not covered, so if pattern loss is what brought you here, this section is unlikely to apply to you.

How cranial prosthesis coverage works, and the paperwork to ask for

A cranial prosthesis is not a different product. It is a wig or hairpiece, described the way physicians and insurers describe it. What turns one into the other is the paperwork around it, which is why the single most useful thing you can do is use that term, and not the word “wig”, with both your provider and your insurer.

Coverage varies plan by plan, so the only reliable answer comes from your own plan. Call the number on your card and ask specifically about cranial prosthesis coverage, any annual limit, and whether prior authorization is required before you buy anything.

Come early if you can

Matching is easier while you still have your own hair to match to. If you already know a treatment is coming, one appointment beforehand makes everything afterwards simpler, and we will work around your treatment dates.

A prescription from your provider

It should carry your diagnosis, the diagnosis code your provider assigns, their NPI, and their signature. Ask for it to say cranial prosthesis rather than wig.

A letter of medical necessity

Separate from the prescription, and it strengthens a claim considerably. Ask your provider whether they will write one; many will. That paperwork comes from their office, not from us.

An itemized invoice

We provide this. Ours describes the item as a cranial prosthesis and shows the date, the item and the amount, which is what an insurer or an administrator will ask you for.

Your plan's claim form

Expect to pay up front and file for reimbursement afterwards rather than having it billed directly.

HSA and FSA funds

A prosthesis prescribed for medical hair loss is commonly an eligible expense. Your plan administrator or tax adviser can confirm it for your situation.

Two limits worth knowing in advance. There is no guarantee of reimbursement, whatever the paperwork looks like. And we cannot write the prescription or the letter of medical necessity ourselves; those have to come from your provider. We are a hair replacement studio, not a medical provider: we do not diagnose hair loss, stage it, or prescribe treatment, and nothing on this page is medical advice. If you have not yet seen a doctor about your hair loss, a dermatologist or your primary care provider is the right first stop, and it is worth going before you spend money on anything.
